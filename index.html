Lesson 1: Introduction to Literature

Five questions run through this lesson: what literature is, what it is for, why it matters, what shapes it, and what kinds exist. Answer those five and you know the lesson.

What is literature?

Literature is writing, or storytelling out loud, about being human: what people think, feel and live through. A shopping list is writing, but nobody studies it as literature. A poem about missing someone is literature, because it expresses a feeling.

Say it in the testLiterature is a collection of written or spoken works that express human thoughts, feelings, experiences and ideas, such as novels, poems, plays and essays. In the handout’s words: the written (and sometimes spoken) works produced as a form of expression.

What is it for? The six purposes

Entertaingives enjoyment
Educateteaches knowledge and lessons
Express feelingsputs emotions into words
Criticize societypoints at social problems
Preserve culturekeeps traditions and stories alive
Develop imaginationlets readers picture new worlds

Memory trickSplit them 3 and 3. For the reader’s mind: entertain, educate, develop imagination. For feelings and society: express feelings, criticize society, preserve culture.

Why does it matter? The four reasons

  • It improves language skills.
  • It develops critical thinking.
  • It teaches us about different cultures.
  • It helps us understand human emotions.

Memory trickLanguage, thinking, cultures, emotions.

What shapes literature? The six influences

Writers do not write in an empty room. What is around them ends up on the page.

InfluenceWhat it means / quick example
CultureTraditions and customs. A folktale that carries a community’s customs.
ReligionBeliefs and values. A story built on a moral belief.
HistoryEvents. A novel set during a war.
SocietyPeople and social problems. A story about poverty or inequality.
PoliticsPower and laws. A novel about a government that controls its people.

Forms and genres the part students mix up

Two separate questions sort every text. Keep them apart and the lesson becomes easy.

Question 1: is it real or imagined?Fiction: imaginary characters and events (novel, short story, fairy tale). Nonfiction: real people, facts and events (biography, memoir, essay).
Question 2: how is it written?Prose: ordinary sentences and paragraphs. Verse: poetic lines, often with rhythm and rhyme. Drama: written to be performed.
FormExamples
ProseNovel, short story, essay, biography, autobiography
VerseSonnet, ballad, free verse, lyric poetry
DramaTragedy, comedy, tragicomedy

Watch outOne text answers both questions. A novel is fiction (imagined) and prose (sentences). An essay is nonfiction and prose. A tragedy is drama. If you only answer one question, you lose marks.

More from the handout worth a mark

NonfictionFactual, non-imaginary writing meant to inform, educate or demonstrate. Examples: political essays, biographies.
FictionCreative works with invented stories: imagined events, people and places. The writer may be influenced by real life or rely entirely on imagination. Examples: novels, short stories, plays.
TragedyFocuses on the conflicts of a tragic hero, with strong emotional engagement, and ends with a serious, often sad outcome.
ComedyMany characters and their adventures; offers political, social or religious criticism; ends in a playful, light and often happy way.

Verse is arranged in lines (each line is a “verse”) and is used in poems and in religious texts such as the Quran and the Bible. It has a pattern, a rhythm and sometimes a rhyme.

Lesson 2: Elements of Fiction

Picture a film. Characters are the cast. Setting is the location. Conflict is what goes wrong. Plot is the scenes in order. Point of view is whose eyes you watch through. Theme is what you remember afterwards. Those are the six elements.

1. Characters Who?

Characters are the people, animals or imaginary beings who take part in the story. Every character can be described by three separate questions:

How important are they?Main character (the most important) or minor character (a smaller role).
What role do they play?Protagonist (the hero) or antagonist (the opponent).
Do they change?Dynamic (changes) or static (stays the same).

Example: Cinderella is a main character and the protagonist. Her stepmother is the antagonist, and she stays cruel all the way through, so she is static. Scrooge in A Christmas Carol starts mean and ends generous, so he is dynamic.

Watch outOne character can carry a label from each question. The labels do not compete, because each answers a different question.

2. Setting When and where?

Setting is the time and place of the story.

  • Time: past, present or future.
  • Place: village, city, forest and so on.

“A small village long ago” is a complete setting. Setting also creates a mood: a dark forest at night feels scary before anything happens.

3. Conflict What is the problem?

Conflict is the main problem or struggle in the story. No problem, no story. To name it, ask: who or what is the character fighting?

TypeFighting…Example
InternalthemselvesA boy cannot decide whether to confess a lie.
Character vs characteranother personHero against villain.
Character vs societyrules or the groupA girl fights an unfair law.
Character vs naturethe natural worldA hiker survives a blizzard.
Character vs technologymachinesA crew against a runaway computer.

Internal means inside the character. The other four are external, which means the problem comes from outside.

From the handout: external problems include an enemy, survival and difficult life conditions. Internal struggles include guilt, fear and doubt.

4. Plot What happens?

Plot is the sequence of events. It always moves through five stages. Here they are with one story: a girl, Lina, finds a lost wallet and returns it.

  1. Exposition: the introductionWe meet Lina and see her ordinary day. She finds a wallet.
  2. Rising action: the problems growThe wallet is full of money. She is tempted to keep it.
  3. Climax: the turning pointShe decides to return it. The story turns here.
  4. Falling action: the problem is solvedShe finds the owner and gives it back.
  5. Resolution: the endingThe owner thanks her. The story ends happily.

Watch outConflict is the problem. Plot is everything that happens because of it. Also, climax is the peak or turning point, while resolution is the ending. They are not the same stage.

Memory trickExposition, Rising, Climax, Falling, Resolution: Every Rabbit Can Fly Rockets.

5. Point of view Who tells the story?

Point of view is the perspective the story is told from. Look at the pronouns in the first lines.

First person: “I”The narrator is inside the story. “I opened the door.”
Second person: “you”The story speaks to the reader. “You open the door.”
Third person: “he / she / they”An outside narrator. “She went to school.”

Third person comes in two kinds. Limited: the narrator knows the thoughts of one character only. Omniscient: the narrator is all-knowing and can enter every character’s mind.

6. Theme What is the message?

Theme is the main idea or message of the story. Typical themes: love, friendship, courage, justice, hope, education.

Test tipYour handout treats a theme as the central idea or topic (love, friendship, courage) and also as the message the writer wants to convey. The safe answer does both: name the theme, then add one sentence of message, such as “Friendship: true friends stand by each other in hard times.”

Extra detail from your handout worth a mark

  • Major vs minor themes: major themes are the most important topics, repeated throughout a work (or several works). Minor themes are less important and appear briefly in some parts.
  • Not every story has a resolution. Some have an open ending or a cliffhanger. Resolution is also called denouement.
  • Climax: the highest point, where tension is at its maximum. It often changes the characters’ fate or reveals something hidden.
  • Setting can include season, scenery, buildings and weather. It sets the mood and helps us understand the themes.
  • Protagonist and antagonist: the antagonist is whoever stands against the protagonist, as a direct enemy or as a force that blocks their progress.
  • Any character counts, important or trivial, and characters can be people, animals or even animated objects.

SymbolismIt is on the program but not in your handout, so expect at most a short question. A symbol is an object, person, place or color that stands for a bigger idea. A dove stands for peace; a dark storm can stand for danger or sadness.

Lesson 3: The Short Story

Nothing new to memorize here. This lesson takes the six elements from Lesson 2 and applies them to one kind of text.

What is a short story?

A short story is a brief fictional narrative with a limited number of characters and a focused plot. The lesson’s example is “The Gift” by O. Henry (its full title is “The Gift of the Magi”).

Say it in the testA short story is a brief fictional narrative with a limited number of characters and a focused plot.

Five characteristics

  • Short length.
  • Few characters.
  • One central conflict.
  • Focused on one theme.
  • A surprising or satisfying ending.

That is why it is “short but powerful”: with one conflict and one theme, every detail has a job.

Five types

TypeIt is about
FictionalImaginary events
RealisticEveryday life
FantasticThe supernatural or magical
DetectiveMystery and crime
SatiricalHumor that is also critical

Quick match: ghosts and magic = fantastic. A crime to solve = detective. Ordinary life = realistic. Funny but criticizing society = satirical.

Analyzing a short story: use the six elements

When the test gives you a story, answer the six questions in this order. It is a ready-made structure for your answer.

  1. Characters: who is the protagonist? Is anyone an antagonist? Who changes?
  2. Setting: what time and what place?
  3. Conflict: internal or external? Which type?
  4. Plot: name the five stages with one event each.
  5. Point of view: first, second or third person? Limited or omniscient?
  6. Theme: the message, written as a sentence.

PracticeThe Write tab has a short story with a full model analysis. Try it on paper before you open the answer.

Lesson 4: Introduction to Figurative Language

Figurative language is expressive, imaginative language that creates vivid pictures and emphasizes an idea. “Her smile was sunshine” does not mean a real sun. It paints a picture of a bright, warm smile. There are seven figures to know.

Metaphor A is B

A direct comparison without “like” or “as”. “Time is a thief.” Time is compared to a thief.

TestDoes it say one thing is another thing? Then it is a metaphor.

Simile like / as

A comparison using “like” or “as”. “She is as kind as an angel.”

TestLook for the words like or as.

Personification things act like people

Giving human qualities to non-human things. “The wind whispered.” Only people whisper.

TestIs a non-human thing doing something only a person can do?

Oxymoron opposites together

A combination of contradictory terms. “Bitter sweet”: sweet and bitter together. Another one: “deafening silence”.

TestTwo opposite words sitting side by side.

Hyperbole bigger

An extreme exaggeration for emphasis. “I’ve told you a million times!” It is an exaggeration, not a real number.

TestIs something blown up far beyond the truth?

Understatement smaller

A deliberate understatement for effect. “It’s just a little problem,” said when the problem is actually big.

TestIs something made to sound much smaller than it really is, on purpose?

Synecdoche part for whole

Using a part to represent the whole, or the whole to represent a part. “All hands on deck!” means all people. “A hundred sails” means a hundred ships.

TestIs a small piece standing in for the whole thing?

Which one is it? Ask in this order

  1. Compares two things with like or as? Simile. Says one thing is another, no like or as? Metaphor.
  2. A non-human thing acts or feels like a person? Personification.
  3. Number or size blown up? Hyperbole. Shrunk on purpose? Understatement.
  4. Two opposite words side by side? Oxymoron.
  5. A part stands for the whole (or the reverse)? Synecdoche.

Watch outMetaphor says A is B, two different things. Synecdoche uses a piece of the same thing (sails are part of a ship). Hyperbole and understatement are opposites. If two labels seem to fit, choose the one the lesson uses: “Time is a thief” is a metaphor.

Interpreting a figure in context

The lesson’s skill is explaining what a figure means in the text. Use four steps: quote it, name it, say what it really means, say its effect.

Model answer“Time is a thief” is a metaphor. Time is compared directly to a thief, without like or as. Time is not a person, but it takes moments from our lives without us noticing. This makes the idea vivid and a little sad.

The handout examples learn these too

FigureExample from the handout
Metaphor“This soldier is a true lion.” (shared quality: courage)
Simile“He is strong as an ox.”
Personification“The sun stared at us.”
Hyperbole“I’m dying of thirst.” (not literal; shows how thirsty)

Synecdoche“Teaching young brains.” Brains stand for students, stressing intelligence.

MetonymyThe program title says “metonymy/synecdoche”, but your handout covers only synecdoche. Metonymy replaces a thing with something closely linked to it: “the crown” for the king, “the press” for journalists. Synecdoche is the part-for-whole kind.

Written practice

Answer on paper first, in full sentences. Then open the model answer and compare. Every model follows the same shape: term, definition, example.

1. Define literature and give three of its purposes.

Literature is a collection of written or spoken works that express human thoughts, feelings, experiences and ideas. Examples are novels, poems, plays and essays.

It serves several purposes. It entertains readers, it educates them, and it lets people express feelings. It can also criticize society, preserve culture and develop imagination.

2. What is the difference between fiction and nonfiction? Between prose, verse and drama?

Fiction is based on imaginary characters and events, for example a novel, a short story or a fairy tale. Nonfiction is based on real people, facts and events, for example a biography, a memoir or an essay.

Prose is ordinary writing in sentences and paragraphs. Verse is writing in poetic lines, often with rhythm and rhyme, such as a sonnet or a ballad. Drama is a literary work written to be performed, such as a tragedy or a comedy.

The two pairs answer different questions, so one text can belong to both. A novel is fiction and prose.

3. Name and explain the six elements of fiction.

Characters are the people, animals or imaginary beings who take part in the story. Setting is the time and place. Conflict is the main problem or struggle. Plot is the sequence of events. Point of view is the perspective from which the story is told. Theme is the main idea or message.

The elements work together to build a complete story.

4. Explain the five stages of plot with an example.

Plot is the sequence of events in a story. Take the example of a girl who finds a lost wallet and returns it.

In the exposition we meet the girl and she finds the wallet. In the rising action the problem grows: she is tempted to keep the money. The climax is the turning point, when she decides to return it. In the falling action the problem is solved as she finds the owner. The resolution is the ending: the owner thanks her and the story ends happily.

5. Explain internal and external conflict.

Conflict is the main problem or struggle in a story. Internal conflict happens inside the character, for example a boy who cannot decide whether to confess a lie.

External conflict comes from outside the character. It has four types: character vs character, character vs society, character vs nature and character vs technology.

6. What is a short story? Give its characteristics and types.

A short story is a brief fictional narrative with a limited number of characters and a focused plot. “The Gift” by O. Henry is an example.

Its characteristics are short length, few characters, one central conflict, a focus on one theme, and a surprising or satisfying ending. Its types are fictional, realistic, fantastic, detective and satirical.

7. Define figurative language and explain three figures of speech.

Figurative language is the use of expressive and imaginative language to create vivid pictures and emphasize an idea. For example, “Her smile was sunshine” does not mean a real sun, but a beautiful comparison.

A metaphor is a direct comparison without like or as, as in “Time is a thief.” A simile is a comparison using like or as, as in “She is as kind as an angel.” Personification gives human qualities to non-human things, as in “The wind whispered.”

8. Compare metaphor with simile, and hyperbole with understatement.

Both metaphor and simile compare two things. A simile uses like or as (“as kind as an angel”). A metaphor states the comparison directly (“Time is a thief”).

Both hyperbole and understatement change the size of something for effect, in opposite directions. Hyperbole is extreme exaggeration for emphasis (“a million times”). Understatement deliberately says less than the truth (“just a little problem”, said about a big one).

10. Analyze this story

Read it, then answer the six questions plus the figures of speech.

Lina found the leather wallet beside the bus stop, thick with money. Her own pockets were empty, and rent day was close. All evening she carried it like a stone in her coat. Her heart argued with itself: keep it, or return it? At dawn she walked across town to the address inside. The old man who opened the door cried with relief. “Honesty,” he told her, “is the richest thing you carry.” Lina walked home lighter than she had left.

Model analysis

Characters: Lina is the main character and the protagonist. The old man is a minor character. There is no real antagonist.

Setting: a present-day town, at the bus stop and at the old man’s door, from evening to dawn.

Conflict: internal. Lina is torn between keeping the money she needs and returning it.

Plot: Exposition: Lina finds the wallet. Rising action: she carries it all evening and is tempted. Climax: she decides to walk across town to return it. Falling action: the old man cries with relief. Resolution: she walks home lighter.

Point of view: third person limited. The narrator says “she” and shows only Lina’s thoughts.

Theme: honesty is worth more than money.

Figures: “like a stone in her coat” is a simile. “Her heart argued with itself” is personification. “Honesty is the richest thing you carry” is a metaphor.
